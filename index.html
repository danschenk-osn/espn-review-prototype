The app doesn't.

I check scores daily. I follow multiple teams across multiple sports. I play fantasy football, and I'd play fantasy baseball if anyone at ESPN had ever bothered to ask. If they designed their ideal user, I'd be it. So why, when I want a highlight, do I open X?

/ 01 The North Star Problem

The metric you optimize for is the product you build.

One scroll session. Friday morning. Not curated:

Evidence · One session · One user
01 Three identical Capital One ads, back to back.
02 A 15-second pre-roll on a 21-second highlight.
03 Walking shoes for elderly users with poor balance, served to a Braves fan.

The product tells you what it's optimized for. The North Star is ad impressions per session. The North Star that builds a stronger business is time on content the user came for.

→ The metric ESPN built

01
Problem · Personalization
The hero block

Not your sport. Not your team. ESPN chose this.

The app opens on a hockey clip. I follow MLB, NFL, NCAAF. ESPN knows. ESPN chose this anyway. Every default is a product decision. This one is the loudest.

02
Problem · Monetization
First ad placement

An ad. Before a single piece of your content.

The Braves walked off in the 10th last night. The second thing the app shows me is an Alfa Insurance ad. Three ads will pass before my team appears anywhere.

03
Problem · Information density
Top Headlines

Nine headlines. Zero labels.

Two NBA, two golf, one soccer, one hockey. I have to read every line to find out if it matters. A sport tag would fix this in a day.

04
Problem · Hierarchy
Your favorites

Your favorites. Buried.

The row that says "we know who you are" lives below a hockey clip, an ad, and nine unlabeled headlines. If favorites were the organizing principle, the whole product would look different.

05
Problem · Ad targeting
Mobile game placement

Monopoly GO. On a sports app.

Three ads down I'm being sold a Hasbro mobile game. Later, walking shoes for elderly users with poor balance. The targeting isn't broken. It's turned off. Impression revenue doesn't require it to be on.

06
Problem · Negative preferences
NCAA Softball

I have never watched college softball.

A sport I've never opened. ESPN has no concept of negative preferences: you can favorite, you can't unfavorite. No downvote. No "not interested."

07
Problem · Real estate
Premier Lacrosse League

Premier Lacrosse League. On my home screen.

A full scoreboard for a league I've never watched, favorited, or searched for. The home screen is the most valuable real estate in the app. That's a statement of priorities.

08
Problem · Subscription mismatch
MLB scoreboard

The first game shows MLB.TV.

I have DirecTV. The Braves are on Braves Vision tonight, included with my plan. ESPN knows. The app shows MLB.TV anyway. This isn't a UX bug. It's a trust failure.

09
Problem · Surfacing
Your team

Braves won in 10 last night. Here they are, fifth.

Past three ads, a mobile game, softball, lacrosse, and four other MLB games, the Braves are fifth. No Highlights button. And ESPN, who owns Fantasy Baseball, never once asked this fantasy-football-playing Braves fan to try it.

Bottom line Nine problems. Zero new features required.
End of walkthrough

/ 04 Three Fixes

Three product decisions. No new features.

No new infrastructure. No new data. A decision about what the home screen is for.

01 Decision · Personalize the surface

Make the home screen use what you already know.

The favorites system, subscription data, and viewing history already exist. Using them is a product decision, not an engineering one. My Games leads. Verts defaults to Following. Every headline carries a sport label.

→ Cost to ship
One DRI. One quarter. Measure session depth before and after. If it doesn't move, the thesis is wrong and we learn it cheaply.
02 Decision · Honor the contract

Surface the subscription the user has. Not the one ESPN wants to sell.

ESPN asked for the cable provider at signup. That data should drive every viewing option shown. Show "Braves Vision (included with DirecTV)" first. Show "MLB TV (subscription required)" with a label, or drop it entirely. Collecting data and ignoring it isn't neutral.

→ The metric to watch
Click-through to successful stream. The number is in ESPN's warehouse right now. It is not flattering.
03 Decision · Promote what you own

Promote your own products to the people who would obviously use them.

A Braves fan who plays fantasy football and has no fantasy baseball account, in baseball season, is the most obvious conversion in the product. ESPN buried the prompt in the last section of the last tab. That's not a discovery problem. It's a prioritization failure.

→ The business case writes itself
Promoting Fantasy Baseball to a baseball fan in baseball season isn't aggressive. It's paying attention.
/ 05 The Verdict
"
ESPN isn't losing its best users because it's missing features. It's losing them because it refuses to use what it already knows.
The data is there. The products are there. The users have told you what they want. Build the home screen like you believe them.
End of Review
